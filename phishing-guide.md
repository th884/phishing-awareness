# Phishing Awareness Guide

## What is phishing?

Phishing attack is a type of social engineering whereby a criminal will 'pretend' to be someone you trust so that they can get to provide them with information, open a dangerous attachment or send money 

## Common types

Email phishing: deceptive emails sent to many people
Spear phishing: message tailored to a particular person or organization
Smishing: phishing through SMS or messaging app
Vishing: phishing through voice calls

Modern phishing can be convincing and may use correct spelling, personal details, and familiar branding. Treat unexpected requests for action carefully and verify them through a separate, trusted channel (NIST, n.d.; FTC, n.d.).

## A safe way to check

1. Do not respond, click on any links, open attachments or scan any codes until you are sure.
2. Consider the context. Are you expecting to receive this? Does it make sense?
3. Do your own research. If they have left you a phone number, website or chat link contact them using a known genuine source. Don't use any details or links provided in the suspicious message.
4. Report using your organisations system. Report any suspicious messages to the nominated IT/security team. Follow any local processes to either keep or delete the message.
5. Safely delete. Once reported and following your organisations instructions, delete or quarantine.

CISA’s awareness guidance promotes recognizing and reporting phishing; the FTC also recommends independent verification and reporting suspicious messages 

## If you already interacted with it

Act quickly. Don’t be embarrassed, quick action will reduce any damage.

If you clicked on a link or opened a file:
• Only disconnect from a network if instructed to by your organisation. Contact IT/Security using a trusted method.
• If you entered a password, contact IT/Security and change your password using the real service or approved method. Change your password anywhere that it has been reused. Do NOT click on a password reset link within the malicious communication.
• If you shared a verification code, contact IT/Security or the service you were affected by. Never share any more codes.
• If you approved a login/MFA prompt that you didn’t initiate, contact them immediately and follow the account recovery process from a trusted source.
• If you sent money/payment details, contact your bank/payment provider immediately using their official app or verified number.
• If you shared identity details, use the appropriate identity protection or fraud reporting service in your location. In the United States, use the process provided by the FTC (IdentityTheft.gov) (FTC, n.d.).
• Save any relevant evidence following your organisations instructions. Do not share any suspected malware or personal information.
## Safer habits

Use unique passwords and password managers.
Ensure you have MFA enabled for important accounts. While extra verification is useful, if you see unexpected prompting for authentication then you should refuse it and report.
Make sure devices, browsers and security programs are up-to-date.
If there is an option to report spam/phishing make use of it.
Raise any security concerns you may have. It can be useful to report close calls.
